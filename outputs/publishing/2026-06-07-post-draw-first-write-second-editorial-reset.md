# A Picture Is Worth A Thousand Words At The USPTO.

*Human Authored · AI Assisted*

Part of Andrew Leung's startup patent strategy series at W&Patent. Read the flagship guide first for the broader founder framework.

## Possession Comes First

Founders are often told to focus on writing a detailed description.

Andrew's view is that the earlier question is whether the filing shows what the founder actually possessed on day one.

That is the practical heart of the issue.

If the disclosure is vague, future flexibility is weaker.
If the disclosure is concrete, the founder has more places to go when broad claims meet prior art.

## Why Drawings Beat Dense Prose

This is where founders often misunderstand what makes a provisional strong.

A long document can still be vague.
A simple drawing can still be concrete.

Andrew's point is not that prose does not matter.
It is that founders often underrate how much visual specificity helps:

- show structure
- show variation
- show relationships between parts
- show what the founder actually had in mind

That is why drawings often carry more strategic value than founders expect.

## What Founders Usually Miss

The common founder mistake is treating the provisional like a school paper:

- longer sounds safer
- legal tone sounds stronger
- abstraction sounds broader

Andrew's view is that this can backfire.

If the filing becomes abstract without becoming concrete anywhere, the founder may have less useful fallback material later.

What founders usually miss is that a clearer original disclosure can create more future flexibility than a wordier but fuzzier one.

## Draw First. Write Second. Claim Third.

Andrew's founder-language sequence is:

1. draw first
2. write second
3. claim third

Why this order works:

- the drawing forces specificity
- the writing explains what the founder actually meant
- the claims then sit on top of something more concrete

This is not a rule for every edge case.
It is a better default for founders than starting with dense legal prose and hoping it captures the real invention later.
